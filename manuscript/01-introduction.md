# Introduction: The Paycheck Question

One evening not long ago, I did something I'd advise you to do only when you're sitting down. I took my own job description, the real one, the document my employer uses to decide what I am paid to do, and scored it line by line against a published index of which work tasks current AI models can already perform.

I have spent nine years in offices: executive support, analytics, research administration. I am a professional doer of exactly the kind of tasks you will meet throughout this book. Scheduling, drafting, summarizing, reconciling, reporting. I did not like my score. We'll come back to it in Chapter 11, where you'll compute your own.

What that evening actually taught me was something else. My discomfort wasn't the number. It was realizing how little of the public argument about AI and jobs would have helped me interpret it. For a decade, the debate has run between two camps that agree on almost nothing except their own certainty. One says the machines are coming for half of all work. The other says relax, technology always creates more jobs than it destroys. Both camps spent most of that decade arguing without measurements.

## The forecast graveyard

We have been here before, and recently.

In September 2013, two Oxford researchers, Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne, published a working paper estimating the probability that 702 occupations could be computerized. Their headline finding: about 47 percent of total US employment was at risk.¹ The number detonated. It appeared in parliamentary hearings, boardroom decks, and a thousand dinner-party arguments. It is probably the single most famous statistic ever produced about automation.

A decade later, the almost-half-of-all-jobs apocalypse had not arrived. By the time the researchers you'll meet in Chapter 2 examined US payroll records in 2026, total employment had grown through the first three and a half years of the generative-AI era.² Economists at the OECD, re-examining the question with a task-based method in 2016, had put the share of jobs at high risk of automation at roughly 9 percent for the United States, not 47.³ Two serious teams, same question, estimates five times apart. That is what guessing looks like when it wears equations.

I want to be fair to Frey and Osborne. They measured what was technically plausible, and the press reported it as a forecast of job losses. That distinction, between what a machine could do and what then actually happens to employment, was lost immediately, and the public conversation never recovered it.

This book exists to recover it.

## Exposure is not displacement

Here is the distinction. It is the spine of every chapter that follows.

**Exposure** is a statement about tasks on paper: given what AI can currently do, what share of the tasks in this occupation could it perform or meaningfully speed up? Exposure is a weather forecast. It tells you where rain is possible.

**Displacement** is a statement about people in records: are fewer humans actually employed, hired, or paid in this occupation than we would otherwise expect? Displacement is the rain gauge. It tells you what actually fell.

Almost every frightening number you have heard (47 percent, "half of entry-level jobs in five years," "300 million jobs") is an exposure number dressed up as a displacement claim. And almost every soothing argument ("the ATM created teller jobs," "technology always nets out positive") is a displacement story from a previous technology, deployed to dismiss exposure evidence from this one. The two camps are not even arguing about the same quantity.

## Why this book is possible now

Until very recently, an honest book on this subject could not have been written, because the rain gauges didn't exist. Between 2023 and 2026 they arrived, and this book is a tour of them:

- **Task-level exposure indices** built from the US government's catalogue of what every occupation actually does. One comes from researchers at OpenAI and the University of Pennsylvania, one from the International Labour Organization (Chapter 1).⁴
- **Payroll records** covering millions of workers month by month, which revealed the first credible displacement signal of the AI era. It was not where anyone was pointing (Chapter 2).
- **Job postings**, the labor market's intention data, scraped and indexed in near-real time (Chapter 3).
- **Usage logs**: what millions of people actually do with AI, published by an AI company itself and mapped to occupational tasks (Chapter 4).
- **Randomized experiments** that measure what happens to real workers' output when you hand them the tool (Chapter 8).

Part I reads these instruments one by one. Part II uses them to name names: the occupations in the path of the storm, the ones in the clear, and the surprising group for whom AI is a raise in disguise. Part III is about what to do. What history's automation waves actually teach (Chapter 10), how to score your own job in thirty minutes with a pencil (Chapter 11), what to tell your kids (Chapter 12), and a 30-day plan that starts Monday (the Toolkit).

Along the way you will meet one number so important that it gets its own chapter, and so misused that I will spend as much ink on what it does not mean as on what it does. It is the reason this book opens with a payslip and not a robot.

Nineteen percent. Chapter 2 is where it came from. Chapter 1 is what you need to know first: to see what AI actually takes, stop looking at jobs and start looking at tasks.
