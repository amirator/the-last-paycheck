# Chapter 8: The Superworkers

In 2020, a Fortune 500 software company did something that would accidentally produce the most important workplace experiment of the AI era. It began rolling out an AI assistant to its customer support agents. Thousands of them, in staggered waves, and the staggering is the part that matters: a staggered rollout is a natural experiment. Compare the agents who have the tool with the statistically identical agents who don't have it yet, and you can measure what the machine actually does to human output, cleanly, in the wild.

Erik Brynjolfsson (yes, him again), with MIT's Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond, got the data: 5,179 agents. The study, "Generative AI at Work," became the first field experiment of its kind published in a top economics journal.¹

The average result was striking enough. Agents with the AI assistant resolved about 14 percent more issues per hour.¹ But the average was hiding the real finding, and the real finding reorganizes how you should think about everything in this book.

The newest, least-skilled agents improved about 34 percent. The most experienced, most skilled agents improved barely at all.¹

A decade of intuition says that should be backwards. The tool didn't amplify the experts. It cloned them. The researchers' interpretation: the AI had, in effect, bottled the best practices of the top performers (the phrasings that calm angry customers, the diagnostic shortcuts) and handed the bottle to the novices. The veterans gained little because the machine was serving their own distilled experience back to them. The novices moved down the experience curve in months instead of years. Customer sentiment improved. Attrition fell.¹ Along one more dimension the gap between the best and the rest, the skill premium that structures careers, pay, and status, compressed.

## Three experiments, one shape

Call-center work is one occupation. The remarkable thing is that the same shape showed up wherever economists ran controlled experiments in the early wave.

**Writers.** MIT's Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang, in *Science*: 453 college-educated professionals doing realistic writing tasks like press releases, reports, and delicate emails. With ChatGPT, about 40 percent faster, with output judged about 18 percent higher quality by professionals in the same occupations. The quality gains were largest for the writers who had scored worst without it; inequality between workers decreased.²

**Elite consultants.** The jagged-frontier experiment: 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants, with Harvard, Wharton, and MIT researchers, on 18 tasks designed to mirror real consulting work. With GPT-4, 12.2 percent more tasks completed, 25.1 percent faster, quality rated around 40 percent higher. Below-average performers gained about 43 percent; top performers about 17.³ The same compression, among some of the most credentialed workers on earth.

Novices up a third. Weak writers closing the gap. Bottom-half consultants nearly catching the top. The machine is a skill leveler. For everyone standing on experience and polish as their moat, that is an uncomfortable gift. For everyone locked out of the experience factory of Chapter 6, it is the most hopeful number in the book, and it is the ladder problem's partial antidote: what the machine takes from the bottom rung as practice, it partially returns as performance.

## The jagged edge

The BCG study carried a second finding that should be stapled to the first, because it is the failure mode of the superworker era.

The researchers also designed a task deliberately placed just outside the AI's capability, one where the model would produce a confident, fluent, wrong answer. On that task, consultants using AI performed roughly 19 percentage points worse than consultants without it.³ The tool's authority didn't switch off at the boundary of its competence. The humans stopped checking and, as the lead author put it, fell asleep at the wheel. AI capability is a jagged frontier, superhuman on one task and subtly wrong on a neighboring task that looks identical, and almost nobody can see where the edge runs without testing it.

Put the two findings together and you get the actual skill of the AI era. Not prompting. Knowing where the frontier is, in your own job, task by task, by testing it, so you delegate inside the edge and verify everything near it. The worker who can do that gets the 34 percent. The worker who can't gets the confident wrong answers at scale.

## Who pockets the gains

One honest asterisk before the chapter ends. Output per worker rising 14, 25, 40 percent is measured fact. Whether pay follows is not yet. The broad wage data shows no AI windfall for workers so far, and Chapter 2 found adjustment running through headcount precisely because wages are sticky.⁴ Postings offer a 28 percent premium for AI skills,⁵ which is real money for individuals who reposition. But the macro question, who captures the surplus, is the great unresolved issue of the next decade, and this book won't pretend the data has settled it.

What the experiments do settle is this: where AI augments, the gains are large, fast, and biggest at the bottom of the skill curve. Zoom out from the individual workplace, though, and the gains have a geography. The same technology that levels skills within a call center may do something very different between countries. That is next.
