# Chapter 1: Tasks, Not Jobs

Ask a paralegal what they do and you'll get a job title. Ask the US Department of Labor and you'll get a list.

The government's O\*NET database, the occupational census that underpins nearly every study in this book, doesn't describe a paralegal as "a paralegal." It describes the work as tasks: *prepare affidavits and other documents. Investigate facts and law of cases. Organize and maintain documents in paper or electronic filing systems. Meet with clients and other professionals to discuss case details. File pleadings with court clerk.*¹

Now run down that list with an honest eye and today's AI models in mind. Drafting a standard affidavit from case details? A language model does a competent first pass now. Summarizing deposition transcripts? Yes, and faster than any human. Organizing and retrieving documents? Largely yes. But meeting a frightened client and knowing which questions to ask, and which not to ask yet; walking a filing through a particular court clerk's particular preferences; being the person a lawyer trusts when the deadline is tonight? No, and not close.

Same job title, radically different answers, task by task. That is the first lesson of the measurement era: AI does not arrive at the level of jobs. It arrives at the level of tasks. A "job" is just a bundle of tasks that it has historically been convenient to hire one human to do. Technology unbundles, task by task, and then organizations decide what to do with the hours that come free. Everything in this book flows from that.

## Measuring the unbundling

O\*NET catalogues roughly a thousand occupations and around 19,000 distinct task statements.² In 2023, four researchers (Tyna Eloundou, Sam Manning, and Pamela Mishkin of OpenAI, with economist Daniel Rock of the Wharton School) did the obvious-in-hindsight thing. They went through those tasks and asked, for each one, whether a large language model could do it, or meaningfully speed it up, to a usable standard. Then they rolled the task scores back up into occupations.³

The headline findings from "GPTs are GPTs" have been quoted everywhere, usually without the care they deserve, so here they are with it:

- Around **80 percent of US workers** are in occupations where at least 10 percent of tasks are exposed to LLMs.³
- Around **19 percent of workers** are in occupations where at least half of tasks are exposed, once you count tools built on top of the models.³
- With today's models alone, no extra software, their human reviewers put that over-half-exposed group at only about 3 percent of workers.³

Read those three bullets carefully and notice what they are. They are statements about task lists and model capabilities. Exposure, the weather forecast. They say nothing about whether anyone has lost a job. The authors said so themselves, plainly: technical feasibility does not guarantee automation outcomes.³ The careless reading ("80 percent of jobs affected by AI!") ran around the world anyway.

Two years later, the International Labour Organization, working with the Polish research institute NASK, rebuilt the exercise globally with finer task data. Their refined index, published in 2025, found that **one in four workers worldwide** (25 percent) is in an occupation with some degree of exposure to generative AI. In high-income countries, it's **34 percent**.⁴ And the occupational category most exposed of all, consistently, in every income band: **clerical and administrative work**.⁴ Hold that thought; it is Chapter 5.

The ILO attached a warning label in nearly the same breath. These are *potential* exposure figures, they wrote, not expected job losses; transformation of work within jobs is the more likely outcome in most cases.⁴ When the institution that produced the number tells you not to read it as layoffs, believe it.

## What the exposure maps actually show

Lay the indices side by side and a consistent geography emerges.

**Most exposed:** work whose tasks are reading, writing, summarizing, classifying, calculating, and routine communication. Clerical and secretarial roles. Customer service. Bookkeeping. Data entry. Translation. Paralegal work. And, in the finding that broke a decade of career advice, software development, whose task list overlaps heavily with what language models demonstrably do.³ ⁴

**Least exposed:** work whose tasks require hands, physical presence, unpredictable environments, or accountable human judgment. Plumbers, electricians, care aides, construction trades, agricultural work. In Chapter 4 you'll see this confirmed from a completely different instrument, actual AI usage logs, where farming and forestry occupations barely register.⁵

**The twist the averages hide:** exposure rises with wages and education through most of the range. Eloundou and colleagues found higher-income jobs generally facing greater exposure, with science- and critical-thinking-heavy roles the exception.³ The 2013 panic was about robots coming for the warehouse. The 2020s measurements say the language models came for the desk.

One more property of exposure matters before we go anywhere near employment data. Exposure is dual-use by construction. A task a model can do is a task that can be automated away from you, or a task you can now do in a tenth of the time. The index cannot tell you which way an employer will jump. Nothing at the level of tasks-on-paper can. The same 50-percent-exposed job description is a pink slip in one company and a promotion in another.

Which is exactly why this chapter is not enough, and why the loudest voices on both sides, the ones extrapolating straight from exposure scores to apocalypse or to utopia, were always bluffing.

Exposure is a weather forecast. The next chapter is the rain gauge: the first credible measurement of what has actually happened to employment since the storm formed. It is not what either camp predicted.
