# Chapter 10: History's Receipts

In September 1969, at a Chemical Bank branch in Rockville Centre, New York, a machine began dispensing cash without a human behind it.¹ The automatic teller machine was, by any task-based analysis, a direct hit: counting out cash was the core of a teller's day. The forecast wrote itself. Tellers were finished.

Here is what the receipts say happened instead. As ATMs spread through the 1980s and 1990s, the number of tellers a branch needed did fall. Economist James Bessen, who assembled the definitive account, puts it at roughly 21 per urban branch down to about 13.² But a cheaper branch turned out to mean more branches. Banks opened them by the thousand, and total US teller employment rose for decades after the machine arrived, from roughly a quarter million in 1970 to around half a million by 2010.² ³ The job changed inside its own title: less counting cash, more selling, troubleshooting, and relationship work. The tasks the machine couldn't do became the job.² Only in the smartphone era, with mobile deposits and vanishing branches, did teller numbers finally turn down. A different technology, a half-century later.

The ATM story has become the standard-issue reassurance of the AI debate, so let's be precise about what it actually proves. It does not prove that automation never costs jobs. It demonstrates a mechanism: when automating a task makes the service cheaper, demand for the service can expand enough to grow the jobs around the remaining tasks. Whether that mechanism fires depends on the demand being there to unlock. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is not, and history keeps receipts on both kinds.

## Three waves, three verdicts

**The spreadsheet.** VisiCalc shipped in 1979 and did to manual ledger arithmetic what the ATM did to cash-counting. The two occupations touching that task went opposite ways. Bookkeeping and accounting clerks, the people whose job was the arithmetic, went into long decline. Accountants and financial analysts, the people whose job was what the arithmetic was for, multiplied.⁴ Cheaper calculation meant everyone wanted more analysis. The task died; the judgment layer above it boomed. One technology, one task, winners and losers divided by which side of the task they stood on.

**The switchboard.** Telephone operators were one of the great employers of young American women, until direct dialing deleted the job class outright. Economists James Feigenbaum and Daniel Gross traced what happened to the operators. The incumbents, the young women doing the job when the technology hit, took real, measurable losses, ending up in lower-paying work or out of the workforce. The next cohort of young women, who would have become operators, simply flowed into other growing occupations and showed little lasting scar.⁵ The job class died. The demographic recovered. The specific people holding the job at the wrong moment paid the bill.

**The loom.** And the oldest receipt, from the original machine-breakers' era. English textile automation eventually delivered enormous productivity and, eventually, higher wages. But economic historians' reconstructions show roughly a half-century gap in which output soared while workers' real wages stagnated, the stretch Robert Allen named "Engels' pause."⁶ The gains were real. They took two generations to reach the people displaced, which is to say they reached their grandchildren.

## What the receipts actually teach

Lay the waves side by side and the honest lessons belong to neither camp.

*In aggregate, it has always worked out; for individuals, frequently not.* Economy-wide employment grew through every wave, and the displaced-worker literature consistently finds that individual workers pushed out of long-held jobs suffer large, persistent earnings losses, often never fully recovering.⁷ "It worked out before" is true as macroeconomics and false as a promise to any particular 48-year-old bookkeeper. Both halves are in the data.

*The unit of destruction was always the task; the unit of survival was the judgment above it.* Tellers who became relationship-sellers and clerks who became analysts were living the pattern this book keeps finding: the routine layer went to the machine, while the accountable layer stayed human and often grew.

*Timelines ran in decades*, because adoption took that long when it required branches to be built and switchboards to be replaced. Here is the honest asterisk for today: software diffuses faster than bank branches, and Chapter 2 found measurable hiring effects within three years of launch. History's clock may understate this wave's speed. Its mechanisms, so far, are tracking closely.

*The hardest-hit were the young and the new*: the would-be operators, the never-hired clerks. The quiet injury of Chapter 2, visible in 1920s census records. The canary pattern isn't new. We just couldn't see it in real time before.

History's verdict, then. The question was never whether work survives. It does. The question is whether your tasks do, and whether you're standing on the judgment layer or the routine layer when the wave arrives. Which makes the next chapter unavoidable: it's time to score your own job.
