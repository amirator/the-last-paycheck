# Chapter 3: What Job Postings Say

Before a single paycheck changes, somebody writes a document. A manager opens last year's job posting, hits "duplicate," and then edits it. This is the moment that interests us. A requirement gets added. A line gets deleted. Sometimes the whole posting never goes up at all, and the only evidence is an absence.

Job postings are the labor market's intention data. They are noisy. Companies post jobs they never fill, inflate requirements, forget to take listings down. But they have one great virtue: they move first. Payroll tells you what employers did months ago. Postings tell you what they want next. If Chapter 2's door is really narrowing, the postings should show the frame shrinking before the payroll shows fewer people walking through.

They do. Everything in this chapter is date-stamped, because posting data moves monthly. Treat these as readings from mid-to-late 2026, not eternal truths.

## The disappearing junior line

Indeed's Hiring Lab, the research arm of the world's largest job site, tracks postings by seniority. As of May 2026, entry-level postings in the US were down 7.5 percent year over year. Senior postings, over the same stretch, were *up* 14.7 percent.¹ One market, two directions, split exactly along the line the payroll data predicted: experience.

Nowhere is the split starker than in the occupation that spent a decade as the default advice. Software development postings on Indeed have partially recovered from their post-2022 slump, up almost 15 percent from early 2025, but they remain roughly 27 percent below their February 2020 level, and the recovery has a shape: about 71 percent of the May 2025 to May 2026 increase came from senior roles. In the first quarter of 2026, 69.3 percent of US software development postings targeted senior candidates. The share targeting entry-level candidates: 4.5 percent.² The industry is hiring again. It is hiring people it doesn't have to train.

Meanwhile, where does entry-level demand hold up? Indeed's data answers that too: 91.3 percent of personal care and home health postings are entry-level.¹ The front door hasn't closed everywhere. It has moved, away from desks, toward work with hands and presence. Chapter 7 is about that territory.

## What employers now ask for

The second signal in the postings is additive: a requirement spreading through job ads like a dye through water.

Indeed's AI tracker (the data is public, downloadable, and updated continuously) shows AI-related postings climbing to roughly 5.4 percent of all US postings by April 2026, with GenAI-specific mentions having multiplied several-fold since 2024.³ Among software development postings, nearly half now mention AI.²

And employers are paying for it. Lightcast, which analyzes billions of postings, found in its 2025 "Beyond the Buzz" report that postings asking for at least one AI skill advertised salaries 28 percent higher, roughly $18,000 a year, than comparable postings without. Name two or more AI skills and the premium climbed toward 43 percent.⁴ The most striking line in that report isn't the premium, though. It's the location of the demand: 51 percent of AI-skill postings in 2024 sat *outside* IT and computer science, and demand for generative-AI skills in non-tech roles had grown roughly 800 percent since 2022.⁴ The skill stopped being a tech credential and became an office one.

Put the two posting signals together and you get the labor market's intention, stated plainly: fewer juniors, more AI. Not as a slogan. As line items. The junior requisition quietly not duplicated this year; the "experience with AI tools" line quietly added. Neither edit makes the news. Both are visible in millions of postings at once, and both point the same way as the payroll records.

## The honest caveats

Postings deserve the same cross-examination the payroll got. Three cautions.

First, postings measure *advertised* demand, not hiring. Ghost postings, repostings, and requirement inflation all distort levels, which is why this chapter leans on changes and shares. Those are more robust than counts.

Second, attribution is as hard here as it was in Chapter 2. Researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, working with real-time postings data, cautioned in 2026 that it is difficult to attribute the entry-level slowdown to AI alone; junior and senior postings within AI-exposed occupations don't always diverge cleanly.⁵ The posting data is consistent with the canary story. Consistent is not proven.

Third, a posting tells you what an employer wrote, not what the job becomes. The "AI skills required" line, in particular, is partly fashion; some employers sprinkle it to attract applicants.⁴ The premium data suggests many mean it. The sprinkling means you shouldn't treat every mention as a transformed job.

Still: intention data and payroll data, collected by different institutions with different methods and different failure modes, are telling one story with two voices. Employers want fewer beginners and more AI-fluent workers, and they are writing it down in public.

What they do with the tool once the hire is made, whether it replaces the work or multiplies the worker, is the question postings cannot answer. For that you need to watch what people actually do with AI, millions of times a day. Remarkably, that data exists. It's next.
