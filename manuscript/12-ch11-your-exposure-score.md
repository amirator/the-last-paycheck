# Chapter 11: Your Exposure Score

I promised in the Introduction that we'd come back to the evening I scored my own job. This is that chapter. By the end of it, you'll have done the same thing to yours, with a pencil, in about thirty minutes, using the same logic as the academic indices, laid out in the open where you can argue with every step.

The method is a homemade, transparent cousin of the Eloundou and ILO rubrics from Chapter 1. List the tasks, rate each against what current models can actually do, weight by hours.¹ Nothing here is proprietary or clever. That's the point. You should be able to see exactly why your number is your number.

## The worksheet

**Step 1. List your ten core tasks.** Not your job title; your actual week. If you're stuck, look at your calendar and sent folder for the last two weeks, or pull your occupation's task list from O\*NET (onetonline.org) and keep what's really yours. Write the hours per week each one takes.

**Step 2. Rate each task on four questions, 0 to 2 points each.**

- **Q1. Could a current AI model do this task to a usable standard?** (0 = yes, routinely; 1 = partially, with a human finishing; 2 = no.) Be brutal. "Usable" means a competent colleague would accept it as a first draft, not that it's flawless. If you haven't actually tried the task in a current model, your rating is a guess, so try it first. This question is the heart of the Eloundou rubric.¹
- **Q2. Does it require physical presence?** (0 = fully remote-able; 1 = partly; 2 = hands or body required.) The insulation of Chapter 7.
- **Q3. Must a human be accountable for it?** (0 = nobody signs; 1 = someone reviews; 2 = someone's name, license, or neck is on the line.)
- **Q4. How costly is it to verify the output?** (0 = errors obvious or cheap; 1 = middling; 2 = errors subtle and expensive, demanding an expert checker.) This is the jagged-frontier question from Chapter 8. Tasks whose output is hard to verify keep humans in the loop.

**Step 3. Score each task** out of 8. A task scoring 0–2 is *exposed*: a model can do it and little anchors it to a human. A task at 3–5 is *shared territory*: machine first-pass, human finish. A task at 6–8 is *anchored*: yours for the current generation of models.

**Step 4. Weight by hours.** Exposed hours ÷ total hours = your exposure share. That one fraction is your weather report: the share of your paid week sitting in the rain.

## Four honest scorings

**The executive assistant.** My own territory, so no flinching. Calendar and travel: exposed to shared (tools do it; judgment about which meeting matters anchors some). Drafting correspondence: exposed. Meeting minutes: exposed. Expense and document processing: exposed. Gatekeeping and stakeholder handling, knowing who may interrupt the CEO and in which mood: anchored, deeply. Event and crisis logistics with physical presence: anchored. A typical profile lands at roughly half the week exposed, matching the ILO's finding that clerical work is the most exposed category on earth,¹ with the anchored half being precisely the part no task database captures.

**The junior accountant.** Reconciliations, schedules, data entry, first-draft reports: exposed. Judgment calls, client contact, sign-off support: anchored, but thin in hours at the junior rung. A high exposure share, and Chapter 6 explains the hiring data behind it.

**The nurse.** Documentation and handover notes: exposed (and welcome, mostly). Assessment, medication, physical care, de-escalation, being there at 3 a.m.: anchored by presence and accountability. A low exposure share, concentrated in paperwork. The machine threatens the clipboard, not the nurse.

**The sales rep.** Prospect research, outreach drafts, CRM hygiene, proposals: exposed. Discovery conversations, negotiation, trust, quota accountability: anchored. A middle score with a clear playbook: automate the exposed half and sell more with the freed hours. Chapter 8's augmentation dividend, available immediately.

## Reading your number

Three rules for interpretation, each one a chapter you've already read.

**Your score is exposure, not fate.** A high number says the machine could. Whether your employer automates, augments, or ignores is a business decision the worksheet cannot see. (Chapter 1, and remember the ILO's own warning label.¹)

**The 0–2 tasks are your to-do list, not your doom.** Every exposed task is also a task you can now do in a fraction of the time. The worksheet's exposed column, read upside down, is a list of the hours you can reclaim this month. The Toolkit turns it into a plan.

**Watch the trend, not the snapshot.** Your score is dated the day you compute it; frontier capability moves. Re-run the worksheet twice a year. The direction of travel of your own number is more informative than any single reading. (That's the lesson of 13, then 16, then 19.²)

When I scored my own week, the exposed share came out high enough to explain why this book exists. The anchored remainder was a precise description of why I still had the job: the parts where someone has to be trusted, present, or accountable. The worksheet didn't tell me my future. It told me where, in my own week, the negotiation would happen.

Yours will tell you the same. Which raises the question I get at every family gathering, usually over tea, usually about someone's son or daughter choosing a degree. If the exposed tasks are the teachable ones, what should the next generation learn? That's the last chapter's question.
