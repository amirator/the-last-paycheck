# Conclusion: The Next Paycheck

Go back to the payslip on the cover of this book.

The fear that named this book was always an image: the last one. The envelope, the handshake, the job gone to a machine. Twelve chapters of evidence say that image, vivid and cinematic and everywhere in the discourse, is mostly not how this is happening. What the records show instead is quieter and stranger. Paychecks are not vanishing. Their line items are being rewritten, one task at a time, mostly without anyone announcing anything.

Here is what the data, as of its 2026 vintages, actually supports saying. No more, no less.

Supported: AI arrives at tasks, not jobs, and exposure is measurable (Chapter 1). There is no economy-wide displacement in the payroll records; employment grew right through the first years of the generative era (Chapter 2). There is one sharp, corroborated, growing exception: young workers in the most exposed occupations, roughly 19 percent below the path of their less-exposed peers as of June 2026, through hiring rather than firing, concentrated where AI use is automation-shaped (Chapters 2–4). The exposed middle of the office is being reshaped, with women holding a disproportionate share of the highest-risk desks (Chapter 5). Where AI augments, measured productivity jumps, most for the least experienced (Chapter 8). History says aggregates recover, individuals often don't, and the judgment layer above an automated task is where survival happens (Chapter 10).

Not supported: mass unemployment now or on any measured trajectory. "Safe forever" jobs. The comforting claim that nothing is happening. Any confident forecast of 2030. Anyone selling certainty in either direction is selling past the data.

And me? The honest ending is the one I can verify. I ran my own week through the Chapter 11 worksheet, and the exposed half of it told me what to do, which turned out to be the same thing the whole evidence base recommends. Move my hours up the stack, from producing the routine output to checking, deploying, and being accountable for it, and let the machine have the tasks it has already taken anyway. This book is itself part of that move: an analyst's bet that the judgment layer, deciding which numbers deserve trust and what they mean, is work worth being paid for after the drafting layer went to the machines.

The last paycheck, it turns out, is not the one before unemployment. It is the last one you collect for the old version of your job. Almost everyone reading this will collect one, most without noticing the week it happens. The question the data cannot answer, because it belongs to you, is what the next paycheck pays for. Which tasks you'll have let go, which you'll have claimed, whether you found the jagged edge before it found you.

The storm is real, and the gauges are finally working. Turn the page. The first thirty days are already planned.
