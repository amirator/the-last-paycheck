# Chapter 9: Countries and Cities

Two office workers sit at two desks, five thousand kilometers apart, doing recognizably the same job. Invoices in, records updated, reports out, inbox managed.

One works in Frankfurt: fully digital workflow, cloud systems, an employer already piloting AI tools, reliable broadband to her kitchen table. The other works in a mid-sized city in a lower-income country, doing the same tasks on paper forms and local spreadsheets, with patchy connectivity and software several generations old. Run both jobs through an exposure index and the tasks score identically. But exposure is technical possibility, and possibility only becomes reality where the infrastructure, the capital, and the adoption exist to act on it.

Which is why the global map of AI exposure is really a map of income.

## The gradient

The IMF put the numbers on it in January 2024, in the analysis that framed the Davos conversation. About 40 percent of global employment is exposed to AI. In advanced economies, roughly 60 percent. In emerging markets, about 40. In low-income countries, 26.¹ The ILO–NASK index, built task by task rather than top-down, lands on the same slope: 34 percent of jobs with some exposure in high-income countries against 25 percent worldwide.² Rich economies are office economies, and offices are what language models do.

If exposure were simply danger, this would read as rich-world risk. But the IMF's analysis cut the exposed group in half in a way that echoes everything in this book. Of the exposed jobs in advanced economies, roughly half are positioned to be complemented (the augmentation side, Chapter 8's raise in disguise) and the other half to be substituted.¹ Exposure is the whole storm system, rain and harvest together.

The genuinely uncomfortable finding is for the bottom of the gradient. Low-income countries, with 26 percent exposure, look insulated. The same IMF analysis warned that they are also least prepared to capture the gains: the digital infrastructure, the capital, and the skilled workforce needed to turn AI into productivity are exactly what low exposure is made of. The likely result isn't safety. It is missing the upside while the downside arrives anyway through trade, because the exposed office work of poorer countries is often exported office work. The outsourced back office, the offshore call center, the remote junior developer: these sell codified desk-work across borders, which is to say they sell the most exposed product in this book into the markets that are adopting fastest.

## Forecasts, labeled as forecasts

Alongside the measurements, one widely quoted set of expectations belongs here, properly labeled. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs 2025 survey covered over 1,000 of the world's largest employers, representing 14 million workers across 55 economies. They expect 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030 across all drivers (technology, the green transition, demographics): a net gain of 78 million, with 39 percent of core skills expected to change by then.³ The footnote almost nobody quotes: those totals bundle every trend together, and the AI-and-information-processing slice alone accounts for a far smaller share of both creation and displacement. It is also a survey of what employers say they expect, the same class of data that has been wrong before. Let it calibrate your sense of scale (reshaping, not evaporation) and nothing more. The OECD, for its measured part, puts 27 percent of rich-world employment in occupations at highest automation risk, a figure computed before generative AI was in wide deployment.⁴

## What this means at one desk

Geography, for an individual reader, compresses into three usable facts.

If you work in a high-income economy, your exposure is higher and your augmentation upside is higher. The Chapter 8 playbook applies at full strength, soonest.

If you sell remote office work across borders, the global south's fastest ladder for two decades, you hold concentrated exposure to the automation side regardless of your own country's adoption. The Chapter 11 audit applies to the buyer's market, not yours.

And wherever you sit, the within-country gap may matter more than the between-country one. The same city now contains augmented analysts compounding gains and junior applicants hitting Chapter 2's narrowed door.

The map, like every instrument in this book, describes positions, not destinies. For what positions become, there is only one dataset old enough to answer: the past. Three automation panics have already run their full course, with the receipts preserved. Time to read them.
