# Chapter 4: What People Actually Use AI For

Every day, millions of people open an AI assistant and type what they actually need. Not what they tell surveys. Not what their CEO announced on an earnings call. What they need, at 2:14 on a Tuesday afternoon, with a deadline: *fix this code. Draft this email so it doesn't sound angry. Summarize this contract. Explain this error. Rewrite this paragraph.*

For the first time in the history of automation anxiety, that stream of revealed behavior is being measured and published. In February 2025, Anthropic, the maker of the Claude models, released the first report of its Economic Index: millions of anonymized conversations, mapped onto the US government's catalogue of occupational tasks, the same O\*NET task statements from Chapter 1.¹ Previous technology waves were studied from the outside, by what firms bought. This one can be studied from the inside, by what workers do.

Three findings matter for this book, and each one cuts against somebody's talking point.

## Deep in a few places, shallow almost everywhere

First, usage is concentrated, not universal. In the first report, about 36 percent of occupations showed AI use in at least a quarter of their tasks. Only about 4 percent of occupations showed usage across three-quarters or more of their tasks.¹ Adoption is real and broad at the shallow end. It is still rare at the deep end, where a job's whole task list is being done with the machine.

Where usage concentrates is exactly where the exposure maps of Chapter 1 said it should: software development and writing-heavy work dominate, with usage peaking in mid-to-high-wage occupations. At the other extreme, occupations built on hands and presence barely register. Farming, forestry, and fishing accounted for roughly 0.1 percent of conversations.¹ The weather forecast and the rain gauge agree on the geography.

## The 57/43 split

Second comes the finding this book keeps returning to. The index classifies *how* the AI is used in each conversation. Delegation ("do this task, give me the output") is scored as automation. Collaboration (drafts going back and forth, questions, refinement, learning) is scored as augmentation.

The split, in the first report: 57 percent augmentation, 43 percent automation.¹

Both camps should sit with the half that's inconvenient for them. The majority of real AI use is a person working with the machine, not being replaced by it. And a very large minority, climbing in later releases as agentic tools spread, is flat delegation of a whole task that someone, somewhere, used to be paid to do.²

If the split were just a curiosity of usage logs, it wouldn't deserve a chapter. It earns one because of what Chapter 2 found when the Stanford team laid it against payroll records: young workers' employment fell specifically in occupations where usage skews *automative*, and experienced workers' employment rose with *complementary* usage.³ The 57/43 line isn't a statistic about chatbots. On current evidence, it is the line between the two futures this book's title asks about, and which side of it your occupation sits on is measurable.

## What this instrument can and cannot see

Third, honesty about the lens. The index measures conversations on one company's models, skewed toward early adopters, and it cannot see what happens *after* the conversation: whether the output shipped, whether a task got reassigned, whether a hire didn't happen. Later releases broadened the picture (geography, enterprise API usage, where automation-style use runs notably higher), and parallel studies by Microsoft Research and OpenAI of their own assistants found the same broad shape: information work, writing, coding, and advice at the center; physical work at the edge.² No instrument here sees everything. Every instrument sees the same map.

That map, combined with Part I's other readings, lets us finally stop averaging. "AI's effect on jobs" was always the wrong resolution, like asking about "weather's effect on agriculture." The honest unit is the occupation: its tasks, its usage pattern, its hiring record.

So Part II names names. The exposed middle of the office, where the forecast and the rain gauge now agree. The bottom rung of the ladder, where the door is narrowing. The safe zones, and what safety costs. And the group the doom headlines keep missing: the workers for whom this technology is, measurably, a raise.
