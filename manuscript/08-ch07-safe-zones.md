# Chapter 7: The Surprising Safe Zones

Open a plumber's O\*NET profile next to a financial analyst's and run both through the exposure logic of Chapter 1.

The analyst's task list reads like a language model's résumé: gather data, build models, write reports, prepare presentations, advise on findings. The plumber's reads like a machine's nightmare. Inspect pipes inside a wall nobody has opened in forty years. Diagnose by sound and smell. Crawl into the space, cut, join, test under pressure, reassure the homeowner, leave the kitchen clean. Score it honestly and the plumber's exposure lands near zero.¹ The usage logs agree from the other direction: in the Anthropic index, occupations in farming, fishing, and forestry account for roughly 0.1 percent of AI conversations.² Nobody is delegating pipe-fitting to a chatbot.

After four chapters of exposed desks, this is the other half of the map. The first thing to understand is why it's safe, because the why tells you how durable the safety is.

## What insulation is made of

Across every instrument in Part I, low exposure clusters where work has at least one of four properties.

**Hands in unpredictable places.** Not physicality alone; factory robots conquered predictable physicality decades ago. What resists is physical work in unstructured environments, where every job site is different and every fault unique. Trades, repair, installation, field work.

**Presence.** Work where the body being there is the product: care aides, nurses, physical therapy, childcare, emergency response.

**Accountability.** Tasks where someone must be legally or morally answerable, signing off and bearing liability. A model can draft the inspection report. It cannot be the one whose license is on the line.

**Trust under stress.** The work of being believable to another human at a bad moment: the nurse at 3 a.m., the foreman when the dig hits water.

The US government's projectors see demand stacking up on this side of the map. The largest numeric job growth of any US occupation in the 2024–34 projections belongs to home health and personal care aides: about 740,000 additional jobs, 17 percent growth, on top of what is already the country's largest occupation.³ The fastest percentage growers include wind turbine technicians and solar installers alongside nurse practitioners.³ An aging population and an electrifying grid are hiring hands and presence, right as the exposed middle stops hiring juniors. Recall Chapter 3: over 91 percent of home-health postings are entry-level.⁴ The door that closed on the office side of the market is standing open here.

## What insulation costs

This book promised honesty, so here is the section the "learn a trade" cheerleaders skip.

Safety from AI is not safety from everything, and the insulated zone charges for admission in three currencies. The body: trades and care work carry injury and wear rates office work never sees, and careers that the body, not the market, decides when to end. The paycheck: much of the insulated zone pays modestly. Home health aides, the fastest-growing job in America by headcount, had a 2024 median wage of $34,900, among the lowest of any major occupation, versus $133,080 for software developers.³ The skilled trades sit far above care work, and shortage economics is pushing parts of the trades up, but "insulated" and "well-paid" overlap much less than the pivot-to-plumbing discourse implies. The hours: the work happens where and when the bodies are. Nights, weekends, other people's houses, other people's emergencies.

Two honesty clauses on the safety itself. Insulation is measured against current AI, language models that write and reason but do not yet plumb. Robotics is moving, and this book's method says to watch the task data, not the demos. And no occupation is uniformly safe. The plumber's quoting, scheduling, and invoicing are exposed tasks right now, which mostly means the office half of trade work shrinks while the hands half doesn't. (If you run a one-person trade business, that is good news: the machine wants the part of your week you hate.)

## The quadrant nobody talks about

Lay wages against exposure and the discourse's blind spot appears. The loud panic lives in the high-wage, high-exposure corner: analysts, developers, lawyers. The cheerful advice lives in the low-exposure zones without mentioning the wage axis. Almost nobody talks about the corner that should worry policymakers most, low-wage and high-exposure: the customer service representatives and data-entry clerks of Chapter 5, who have neither the savings to retrain comfortably nor a shortage economy bidding for them.

The map so far is static, though. It shows who is standing where. The more interesting data is dynamic: what happens to a worker's output when the machine arrives at their desk. Because the biggest winners of the AI era, so far, are not the people the machine can't touch.

They're the people using it.
