# Chapter 6: The Juniors' Ladder Problem

Ask anyone who became senior at anything how they learned the trade, and you will hear a description of work that sounds, today, like a language model's job description.

The 1995 junior analyst learned by building the models nobody senior wanted to build, formatting the decks, checking the numbers at midnight. The junior lawyer learned by summarizing depositions and drafting the first pass nobody would ever show a client. The junior developer learned by fixing small bugs in other people's code. The junior journalist rewrote press releases. The junior accountant tied out schedules.

None of this work was glamorous, and almost none of it was the actual product. It was something more important. It was the practice. Every profession has quietly run on the same bargain for a century: juniors do the routine work cheaply, and in exchange, the routine work teaches them the trade. Apprenticeship never died. It moved into open-plan offices and got called "entry-level."

Now re-read that list of junior tasks. Summarizing, drafting, formatting, first-pass building, small-bug fixing. It is almost exactly the task profile that Part I's instruments flag as most exposed. AI eats the practice work that apprenticeship runs on. That is the ladder problem, and it is the mechanism behind the most important number in this book.

## The evidence, assembled

You have already seen the core of it. Here is how the pieces lock together.

From the payroll records (Chapter 2): the young-worker gap operates through hiring, not firing. Separations fell; hiring of 22-to-25-year-olds in exposed occupations is what collapsed.¹ Firms kept the juniors they had trained and stopped training the next ones. The experience paradox is explicit in the data: in the same exposed occupations, often at the same firms, workers over 25 show no comparable gap at all. Every older age band grew while the youngest sat flat.²

From the résumé data: at firms that visibly adopted generative AI, junior employment fell roughly 9 to 10 percent relative to non-adopters from early 2023, while senior employment held. Driven, again, by slower hiring.³

From the postings (Chapter 3): entry-level postings down 7.5 percent year over year while senior postings rose 14.7. Software postings now 69 percent senior-targeted and under 5 percent entry-level.⁴

From the mechanism data: the decline concentrates where AI usage is automation-style, and the knowledge-type analysis says why. A new graduate's inventory is codified knowledge, the kind that is written down and teachable, the kind a model trained on everything written has already absorbed. A veteran's inventory is tacit: judgment, client scar tissue, the unwritten layer. The model substitutes for school and complements experience.⁵

Each instrument has its weaknesses; Chapter 2 cross-examined them. But four independent instruments trace one shape: young, exposed, unhired.

## The seniors' problem in disguise

It is tempting, and plenty of managers have yielded to the temptation, to read all this as efficiency. Why pay a junior analyst to do badly for a week what the model does passably in an hour? On this quarter's numbers, the logic holds. The output gets made either way.

The flaw is arithmetic, and it matures slowly. Every senior was manufactured out of a junior, and the factory was precisely the routine work the model now does. A firm that stops hiring juniors hasn't eliminated a cost. It has deferred one, out past the planning horizon, betting that when today's seniors retire, experience can be bought on the open market. If every firm makes the same bet, the market they're counting on never gets stocked. Economists have a name for what happens to shared resources everyone consumes and no one replenishes.

Note what this argument is not. It is not a prediction of mass graduate unemployment; the cohort's overall employment sat roughly flat, not cratered, because young people kept flowing into less-exposed work.² It is a prediction about the composition of careers: fewer people learning trades through their task lists, in exactly the trades where the task list was the curriculum.

## If the rungs are gone, climb differently

For the reader at the bottom of the ladder, or raising someone who is, the data supports three practical conclusions. Each is developed in Part III.

First, the problem is the codified rung, not youth itself. Entry-level demand is holding where the work has hands and presence: care, trades, field work, the territory of the next chapter.⁴ The door didn't close. It moved.

Second, if practice work no longer comes attached to a paycheck, practice has to be manufactured deliberately. Visible projects, real artifacts, the portfolio that demonstrates judgment rather than certifying coursework. The Toolkit at the end of this book is built around that.

Third, and this is the strangest fact in the whole evidence base, the same technology that eats the practice work can also compress the experience curve for those who do get through the door. In the best-measured workplace deployment to date, the workers who gained most from an AI assistant were precisely the newest ones, who performed like veterans within months.⁶

That finding, the machine as an unequal gift that lands largest at the bottom, gets its own chapter shortly. First, though: if the exposed rungs are dissolving, where exactly is the ground that isn't? Go where the work has hands.
