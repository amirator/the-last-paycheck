# Chapter 12: For Parents

Every family gathering, the same question finds me, usually somewhere between the second cup of tea and the goodbyes. *"So what should my son study? Is computer science still safe? Should she do medicine? What about accounting?"*

The askers deserve better than the two stock answers in circulation: "learn to code" and "the robots will take everything anyway, let him study what he loves." The first has aged badly on the evidence. The second is a forecast wearing a shrug. What the data actually supports is less quotable and more useful. Stop choosing titles. Choose properties. Job titles are how the question arrives; tasks and skills are how the economy will answer it.

## The end of the one-word answer

Start with the advice that defined a generation, because its fate is the whole lesson in miniature.

"Learn to code" was the safest sentence a parent could say from roughly 2010 to 2022. Today, software development sits high on every exposure index,¹ writing code is among the most common uses of AI on earth,² and the entry rung has visibly thinned: entry-level targeting in software postings fell to under 5 percent by early 2026, and young developers are at the center of the Canaries finding.³ Meanwhile the senior end of the same occupation is thriving, and the BLS still projects software development among the largest-growing occupations of the decade.⁴

Hold both halves of that picture, because together they dissolve the question as asked. Coding was never the asset. Coding was the entry ticket, and the ticket is what the machine devalued. The judgment, architecture, and accountability above the code are doing fine. The same split runs through law, accounting, finance, and analysis: the title survives while its teachable bottom rung dissolves. So any one-word answer ("medicine!", "trades!", "AI!") is answering at the wrong resolution. The right resolution is which tasks inside the career, and which rung.

## Skills that compound

If titles won't hold still, what properties should a young person buy? The evidence of this book points to four, each anchored in a finding you've already seen.

**Judgment under accountability.** The tasks that stay human are the ones someone must answer for (Chapters 5–8). Degrees and early jobs that put a young person's name on consequences (clinical placements, audits, site responsibility, client ownership) build the asset the machine can't hold: being answerable.

**Hands-plus-digital hybrids.** The insulated zone of Chapter 7 pays best where it meets technical skill. Medical professionals who run the machines, electricians who do grid-scale and renewables work, the technicians in the fastest-growing percentage occupations in the US projections.⁴ The hybrid is the hedge: presence the models lack, plus the technical layer that lifts the wage.

**Trust and relationships.** The veteran's moat in every dataset, tacit knowledge and client scar tissue (Chapter 2's mechanism⁵), begins as a learnable habit: talking to strangers, selling, caring for people, performing under someone's gaze. Every hour of it compounds into the one inventory the models can't train on.

**Learning speed with the tools.** The superworkers' evidence (Chapter 8) says the gains go to whoever tests the frontier on their own tasks. Employers expect 39 percent of core skills to change by 2030.⁶ At that refresh rate, the meta-skill of re-learning beats any particular syllabus. A young person who graduates knowing how to find the jagged edge of whatever model exists that year carries the one qualification that doesn't expire.

Notice what's absent: any claim that these guarantee anything. This book has spent eleven chapters being honest about what the data can't promise, and it won't stop here. These are the properties the current evidence rewards. Information for a bet every family must size itself.

## What I actually say over tea

So when the question finds me at the next gathering, here is the honest version, compressed.

Don't pick the degree by its title's current safety; by the time she graduates, the exposure map will have moved. Pick for the properties. Does this path build accountability, presence, trust, technical depth, and the habit of re-learning?

Have him run the Chapter 11 worksheet on the occupation he's dreaming about. Not to veto the dream, but so he walks in knowing which rung of it is dissolving and which is load-bearing.

And whatever the field, the entry rung will be the hardest part. Chapter 2 is the proof. So start manufacturing visible, finished work before the door: the portfolio is the new apprenticeship.

Which is, when you think about it, the same advice this whole book has been building toward for the reader's own career. The last pages put it into a calendar.
